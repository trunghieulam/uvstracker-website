The UV index scale, explained

By Trung Hieu Lam, developer of UV Tracker · Published 1 September 2026

What the UV index measures

The UV index is a measure of erythemally-weighted solar irradiance — how strongly sunlight at ground level can cause skin reddening (erythema), the earliest visible sign of sunburn. By definition, 1 UV index unit equals 25 mW/m² of that weighted irradiance. Unlike a percentage or a fixed range, the scale is open-ended: readings above 11 do occur at high altitude and near the equator, and simply mean "extreme" gets more extreme.

The WHO bands

UV indexBandGuidance
0–2LowMinimal protection needed for most skin types.
3–5ModerateSeek shade during midday hours; cover up and wear sunscreen.
6–7HighProtection required — shade, clothing, sunscreen.
8–10Very highExtra care; sunburn risk rises fast on unprotected skin.
11+ExtremeTake all precautions; sunburn risk is high within minutes.

What each level means for your skin

The band tells you roughly how urgent protection is; the exact number of minutes depends on your skin type. At UVI 6 (High), a Type II skin has about 28 minutes of safe sun time; a Type V has about 67. See the full tables by skin type or the calculator for your own numbers.

Why the forecast differs from your sky

Satellite-derived UV index retrievals validate well against ground stations across large regions, but they describe an area, not the specific patch of sky above you right now — clouds move on a much shorter timescale than a forecast update. See the learn section for how a local sky reading adjusts the regional number.

When to check it

The UV index typically peaks within an hour or two of solar noon and falls off sharply toward morning and evening. Checking it once at breakfast can miss a midday spike — the app's sky scan reads the UV where you are, when you ask it to.
